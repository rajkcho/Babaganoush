Best Cashback Credit Cards in Canada (2026 Comparison)

Rick Minji•March 24, 2026•13 min read

Canadians leave hundreds of dollars on the table every year by using the wrong credit card — or worse, a debit card — for everyday purchases. With grocery bills averaging $1,200–$1,500 per month for a family and gas hovering around $1.50–$1.70/litre, the right cashback card can quietly put $500–$1,200+ back in your pocket annually without changing your spending habits at all.

But "best" is relative. The card that's perfect for a single renter spending $2,000/month is completely wrong for a family of four running $6,000/month through their cards. This guide breaks down every major cashback card in Canada for 2026, with real math on who should use what.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • No-fee cards can earn you $300–$600/year — you don't need to pay an annual fee to get meaningful cashback
  • Premium cards ($99–$120/year) are worth it only if you spend $15,000+ per year in bonus categories
  • Grocery and gas are the highest-value categories — look for 4–5% in these areas
  • The card pairing strategy (2–3 cards optimized for different categories) outperforms any single card
  • Welcome bonuses can be worth $150–$400 in the first year — don't ignore them
  • Always pay your balance in full. Even 1% cashback is meaningless against 20.99% interest

Why Cashback Beats Points (For Most People)

Travel points cards get all the blog attention because they sound sexier. "Earn 100,000 Aeroplan points!" sounds more exciting than "Get $350 cash back." But for most Canadians, cashback wins for three reasons:

  1. Transparency: 2% cashback on $100 is $2. Always. Points valuations fluctuate, devalue over time, and require complex redemption strategies to get "full value."
  2. No redemption friction: Cashback hits your statement automatically. Points require logging into portals, searching availability, dealing with blackout dates, and often settling for suboptimal redemptions.
  3. No expiry pressure: Points programs change their terms, devalue their currencies, and sometimes disappear entirely. Cash is cash.
📌The Points Trap

A 2025 Ipsos survey found that 42% of Canadians with rewards credit cards had never redeemed their points. Another 23% redeemed at less than half the "optimal" value (buying merchandise through rewards portals instead of travel). If you're not a strategic points optimizer, cashback gives you better real-world returns with zero effort.

The exception: if you travel internationally 3+ times per year and are willing to learn the points game, a premium travel card can deliver outsized value. For everyone else — cashback.

Best No-Fee Cashback Cards (2026)

These cards cost nothing to hold and still deliver solid returns. If you're starting out, rebuilding credit, or simply refuse to pay annual fees on principle, these are your picks.

Tangerine Money-Back Credit Card

  • Annual fee: $0
  • Cashback: 2% on 2 categories of your choice (3 categories if you have a Tangerine Savings Account), 0.5% on everything else
  • Categories to choose from: Grocery, gas, restaurants, drugstore, entertainment, furniture, transit, recurring bills, hotel/motel
  • Interest rate: 19.95%
  • Best for: People who want to customize their top cashback categories

The math: A household spending $1,200/month on groceries and $300/month on gas would earn $432/year from just those two categories at 2%. Add the 0.5% on everything else and you're looking at $500+ annually — from a free card.

Simplii Financial Cash Back Visa

  • Annual fee: $0
  • Cashback: 4% on restaurants, 1.5% on gas and groceries, 0.5% on everything else
  • Interest rate: 19.99%
  • Best for: People who eat out frequently

The math: If you spend $500/month at restaurants, $1,200/month on groceries, and $300/month on gas, that's $240 + $216 + $54 = $510/year — again, free card.

BMO CashBack Mastercard

  • Annual fee: $0
  • Cashback: 3% on groceries, 1% on recurring bills, 0.5% on everything else
  • Interest rate: 20.99%
  • Best for: Families with high grocery spend

The math: A family spending $1,500/month on groceries earns $540/year from groceries alone. Solid for a no-fee card, though the 0.5% base rate on other spending is weak.

Neo Financial Mastercard

  • Annual fee: $0
  • Cashback: Up to 5% at partner merchants, guaranteed minimum cashback on all purchases
  • Interest rate: 19.99%–24.99%
  • Best for: People who shop at Neo partner retailers (Hudson's Bay, Esso, Mark's, Sport Chek, etc.)
💡Neo's Partner Network

Neo's model is different — they negotiate higher cashback directly with retailers. The catch: the top rates only apply at partner merchants. If your regular stores happen to be partners, it's unbeatable. If not, the base rate is mediocre. Check their partner list before applying.

Best Premium Cashback Cards (2026)

Premium cards charge an annual fee but offer higher category rates and additional perks like insurance, purchase protection, and airport lounge access. The question is always: does the extra cashback cover the fee?

Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite

  • Annual fee: $120 (first year often waived)
  • Cashback: 4% on groceries and recurring bills, 2% on gas and transit, 1% on everything else
  • Insurance: Purchase protection, extended warranty, car rental collision, travel medical ($500K)
  • Income requirement: $60,000 personal or $100,000 household
  • Best for: Families with high grocery and recurring bill spend

The math: A family spending $1,400/month on groceries, $400/month on recurring bills, and $350/month on gas earns ($1,400 × 4%) + ($400 × 4%) + ($350 × 2%) = $672 + $192 + $84 = $948/year. Minus the $120 fee, that's $828 net. Hard to beat.

CIBC Dividend Visa Infinite

  • Annual fee: $99
  • Cashback: 4% on gas and groceries, 2% on dining and Tim Hortons, 1% on everything else
  • Insurance: Purchase protection, extended warranty, car rental, travel medical
  • Income requirement: $60,000 personal or $100,000 household
  • Best for: People who want high rates on both groceries AND gas in one card

The math: Same family: $1,400/month groceries + $350/month gas at 4% = $840/year. Minus $99 fee = $741 net. Slightly less than Scotia Momentum if your recurring bills are high, but the 4% on gas (vs Scotia's 2%) makes up for it if you drive a lot.

Rogers World Elite Mastercard

  • Annual fee: $0 (yes, a World Elite card with no fee)
  • Cashback: 1.5% on everything (no categories), 3% on US-dollar purchases
  • FX fee: 2.5% (effectively 0.5% net cost on USD purchases after the 3% cashback)
  • Income requirement: $80,000 personal or $150,000 household
  • Best for: Cross-border shoppers, online shoppers buying from US retailers, travellers
📌The Best "Flat Rate" Card in Canada

Rogers World Elite is quietly one of the best cards in Canada. The 1.5% flat rate on everything (no categories to track, no caps) with zero annual fee is exceptional. Most flat-rate cards either charge a fee or cap out at 1%. And the 3% on USD purchases makes it the default card for any American website shopping.

Best Cards for Groceries & Gas

These two categories dominate most Canadian household budgets. Here's how the top cards stack up specifically for grocery and gas spending:

Groceries: The Tier List

  1. Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite — 4% (best if you also have high recurring bills)
  2. CIBC Dividend Visa Infinite — 4% (best if you also want 4% on gas)
  3. BMO CashBack Mastercard — 3% (best free option for grocery-heavy spend)
  4. Tangerine Money-Back — 2% (good free option with flexible categories)
  5. Simplii Cash Back Visa — 1.5% (decent, but restaurant rate is the real draw)

Gas: The Tier List

  1. CIBC Dividend Visa Infinite — 4% (best gas rate among major cards)
  2. Canadian Tire Triangle Mastercard — 4¢/litre at Canadian Tire Gas+ (effectively ~2.5–3% at current prices)
  3. Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite — 2%
  4. Tangerine Money-Back — 2% (if you select gas as a category)
  5. Rogers World Elite — 1.5% (flat rate, no category needed)
⚠️Watch the Grocery Definition

Not everything bought at a grocery store counts as "groceries" for cashback purposes. Visa and Mastercard use Merchant Category Codes (MCCs) to classify transactions. Costco, Walmart Supercentre, and some specialty stores may code as "general merchandise" rather than "grocery," which means you'd get the base rate, not the bonus rate. Loblaws, Metro, Sobeys, and Fortinos generally code correctly. Check your statement to confirm.

Welcome Bonus Breakdown

Welcome bonuses can dramatically boost your first-year returns. Here's what the major cards are offering in early 2026:

  • Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite: 10% cashback on all purchases for the first 3 months (up to $2,000 in spend) = up to $200
  • CIBC Dividend Visa Infinite: 10% cashback for 4 months (up to $2,000 in spend) = up to $200
  • BMO CashBack Mastercard: 5% cashback for 3 months (up to $1,500 in spend) = up to $75
  • Tangerine Money-Back: Typically no welcome bonus, but occasional promotional offers through Tangerine
  • Simplii Cash Back Visa: $350 cash bonus after spending $500 in first 3 months (one of the highest flat bonuses available)
  • Neo Financial: $25 welcome bonus — modest, but the card is free
💡Welcome Bonus Strategy

Time your card applications around major planned expenses. Buying new appliances? Getting a car serviced? Renewing insurance? Apply for the card with the best welcome bonus before these purchases, hit the minimum spend requirement, and collect the bonus. Don't manufacture spending just to earn a bonus — that defeats the purpose.

The Card Pairing Strategy

No single card is the best at everything. The optimal approach for maximizing cashback is carrying 2–3 cards, each designated for specific categories. Here's the setup that maximizes returns for a typical Canadian family:

The Two-Card Combo (Simple)

  1. Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite → Use for groceries (4%), recurring bills (4%), and gas (2%)
  2. Rogers World Elite Mastercard → Use for everything else (1.5%) and all USD purchases (3%)

Estimated annual return on $60,000 total spend: $1,050–$1,200 (after Scotia's $120 fee)

The Three-Card Combo (Optimized)

  1. CIBC Dividend Visa Infinite → Groceries (4%) and gas (4%)
  2. Simplii Cash Back Visa → Restaurants and dining (4%)
  3. Rogers World Elite Mastercard → Everything else (1.5%) and USD (3%)

Estimated annual return on $60,000 total spend: $1,200–$1,400 (after CIBC's $99 fee)

The Zero-Fee Combo

  1. BMO CashBack Mastercard → Groceries (3%)
  2. Simplii Cash Back Visa → Restaurants (4%) and gas/groceries overflow (1.5%)
  3. Rogers World Elite Mastercard → Everything else (1.5%) and USD (3%)

Estimated annual return on $60,000 total spend: $850–$1,000 (zero fees paid)

What to Avoid

1. Store Credit Cards

That 10% first-purchase discount at a clothing store sounds great — until you see the 29.99% interest rate. Store cards almost always have worse rates, lower limits, and no useful perks beyond the initial discount. Get the discount, pay it off immediately, then use a proper cashback card going forward.

2. Cards With Cashback Caps

Some cards cap your bonus category earnings at a certain amount per quarter or year. A card offering "5% on groceries up to $500/quarter" sounds amazing, but that's only $25/quarter = $100/year in bonus cashback. Read the fine print and calculate whether the cap limits your actual returns.

3. Carrying a Balance to "Earn" Cashback

This cannot be overstated: if you carry a balance, cashback is irrelevant. Earning 2% cashback while paying 20.99% interest means you're losing 19% per year on that money. Pay your balance in full every month. If you can't, your priority is a low-interest card (10.99%–12.99%), not a cashback card.

4. Too Many Cards

Each credit card application triggers a hard inquiry on your credit report, temporarily lowering your score by 5–10 points. Having 5+ active credit cards also increases your total available credit, which can be a red flag for mortgage lenders even if your utilization is low. Two to three cards is the sweet spot.

⚠️The Annual Fee Trap

Banks know that most people who sign up for premium cards will keep them forever — even when the math stops working. Set a calendar reminder every 12 months to review whether your premium card's annual fee is justified by the cashback and perks you actually use. If you got a first-year fee waiver, the real test is year two.

How to Choose: Decision Framework

Forget the marketing. Answer these four questions:

1. What are your top 3 spending categories?

Pull your last 3 months of bank/credit card statements. Categorize every dollar. For most Canadians, it's groceries → gas/transit → dining/entertainment → recurring bills. Your card should reward your actual top categories, not the ones you aspire to.

2. Can you always pay in full?

If yes → optimize for cashback rate. If no → get a low-interest card first, pay down your debt, then switch to cashback once you're consistently paying in full. Our Debt Payoff Calculator can help you build a plan.

3. Do you spend in USD?

If you shop on Amazon.com, subscribe to US services, or travel to the US regularly, the Rogers World Elite Mastercard should be in your wallet. Most Canadian credit cards charge 2.5% foreign exchange fees — Rogers' 3% cashback on USD purchases effectively gives you 0.5% back instead.

4. Do you value simplicity or optimization?

If you want one card for everything, Rogers World Elite (1.5% flat, no fee) is your answer. If you don't mind using 2–3 cards strategically, the pairing combos above will earn 30–50% more annually.

The Bottom Line

The best cashback credit card in Canada isn't a single card — it's the right card for your spending pattern, ideally paired with one or two others to cover your gaps. Here's the summary:

  • Best overall no-fee card: Rogers World Elite Mastercard (1.5% flat, 3% USD, no fee, no caps)
  • Best no-fee for groceries: BMO CashBack Mastercard (3% groceries)
  • Best no-fee for dining: Simplii Cash Back Visa (4% restaurants + $350 welcome bonus)
  • Best customizable: Tangerine Money-Back (choose your own 2–3 categories at 2%)
  • Best premium for families: Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite (4% groceries + recurring bills)
  • Best premium for drivers: CIBC Dividend Visa Infinite (4% groceries AND gas)
  • Best for cross-border: Rogers World Elite Mastercard (3% on all USD purchases)

Run the numbers on your own spending. The difference between a mediocre card and the right card is $300–$700 per year — money that goes straight into your savings, investments, or emergency fund without requiring you to change a single spending habit.

💡The Golden Rule

Cashback is only valuable if you pay your balance in full every month. If you're carrying credit card debt, ignore everything in this article and focus on paying it off first. A 2% cashback card charging 20.99% interest is the worst deal in personal finance. Get debt-free, then optimize for rewards.
