Parental Leave Finances: Surviving on 55% of Your Income

Rick Minji•April 22, 2026•14 min read

Having a baby is expensive — but most new Canadian parents aren't prepared for the immediate 45% income drop the moment parental leave begins. Employment Insurance pays 55% of your average insurable earnings (capped at $668/week in 2026), while your expenses stay the same or increase. The financial shock is real, and it catches many families off guard.

According to Statistics Canada, 78% of new mothers and 34% of new fathers took parental leave in 2024, but only 32% had a dedicated savings buffer for the income gap. The result? Increased credit card debt, RRSP withdrawals, and financial stress at a time when you should be focused on your newborn.

This guide walks through the real numbers, budgeting strategies, employer top-ups, Quebec's better QPIP system, and how to financially survive (and even thrive) on parental leave.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • EI maternity/parental benefits pay 55% of average insurable earnings, up to a maximum of $668/week ($34,736/year) in 2026
  • Quebec's QPIP pays 70–75% of income (much better than federal EI), but for a shorter period
  • Employer top-ups can bring your income to 75–95%, but they're rare (mostly public sector/large employers)
  • The Canada Child Benefit (CCB) provides up to $7,787/year for your first child — this helps offset new costs
  • Budget for baby costs of $8,000–$15,000 in year one (diapers, formula, gear, medical)
  • Start saving 6–9 months before leave — aim for 3 months of the income gap ($5,000–$10,000 for most families)
  • Use our Net Worth Tracker to monitor your finances during leave

EI Maternity and Parental Benefits (2026)

Canada's federal Employment Insurance provides two types of leave benefits for new parents:

Maternity Benefits

  • Who: Birth mothers only
  • Duration: Up to 15 weeks
  • Payment: 55% of average weekly insurable earnings, up to $668/week
  • Waiting period: No waiting period (eliminated in 2023)
  • Timing: Can start up to 12 weeks before your due date

Parental Benefits (Standard)

  • Who: Either parent (or shared between both)
  • Duration: Up to 40 weeks (combined for both parents)
  • Payment: 55% of average weekly insurable earnings, up to $668/week
  • Total leave: Birth mothers can take 15 weeks maternity + 40 weeks parental = 55 weeks total

Parental Benefits (Extended)

  • Who: Either parent (or shared between both)
  • Duration: Up to 69 weeks (combined for both parents)
  • Payment: 33% of average weekly insurable earnings, up to $401/week
  • Total leave: Birth mothers can take 15 weeks maternity + 69 weeks parental = 84 weeks total
📌Standard vs Extended: Which to Choose?

Standard (55% for 40 weeks) pays more total money than Extended (33% for 69 weeks) — assuming maximum insurable earnings. Standard pays ~$26,720 total; Extended pays ~$27,669 total. The difference is only ~$950 over 29 extra weeks. Choose Standard if you can afford to return to work sooner. Choose Extended if you value the extra time off and can survive on 33% income.

Eligibility Requirements

  • Accumulated 600 insured hours in the 52 weeks before your claim (or since your last claim)
  • Your insurable earnings will have decreased by more than 40% for at least one week
  • You paid EI premiums during your qualifying period

Quebec QPIP: A Much Better System

Quebec runs its own Quebec Parental Insurance Plan (QPIP), separate from federal EI. It's significantly more generous:

QPIP Basic Plan

  • Maternity: 18 weeks at 70% of income (up to $94,000 max insurable earnings)
  • Paternity: 5 weeks at 70% (non-transferable, use it or lose it)
  • Parental (shared): 32 weeks at 70%
  • Total weeks: 55 weeks
  • No waiting period

QPIP Special Plan

  • Maternity: 15 weeks at 75% of income
  • Paternity: 3 weeks at 75%
  • Parental (shared): 25 weeks at 75%
  • Total weeks: 43 weeks

Why is QPIP better? Higher replacement rate (70–75% vs 55%), higher earnings cap ($94,000 vs $63,200), no waiting period, and dedicated paternity leave that can't be transferred. The downside: slightly shorter total duration.

How Much You'll Actually Receive (Worked Examples)

Example 1: $50,000 Salary (Ontario, Standard Parental)

  • Weekly earnings: $961
  • 55% of earnings: $529/week
  • EI benefit: $529/week (below the $668 max)
  • Annual income on leave: $27,508 (vs $50,000 working)
  • Income reduction: $22,492/year (45%)
  • Monthly shortfall: ~$1,874

Example 2: $80,000 Salary (Ontario, Standard Parental)

  • Weekly earnings: $1,538
  • 55% of earnings: $846/week
  • EI benefit: $668/week (capped at maximum)
  • Annual income on leave: $34,736 (vs $80,000 working)
  • Income reduction: $45,264/year (57%)
  • Monthly shortfall: ~$3,772

Example 3: $80,000 Salary (Quebec QPIP Basic Plan)

  • Weekly earnings: $1,538
  • 70% of earnings: $1,077/week
  • QPIP benefit: $1,077/week (below $94,000 cap)
  • Annual income on leave: $56,000 (vs $80,000 working)
  • Income reduction: $24,000/year (30%)
  • Monthly shortfall: ~$2,000
⚠️The Higher You Earn, The Bigger the Gap

If you earn above $63,200 (the 2026 EI maximum insurable earnings), your EI benefits are capped at $668/week regardless of your actual income. Someone earning $120,000 receives the same $668/week as someone earning $65,000 — meaning high earners face an income drop of 70%+.

Employer Top-Ups: The Hidden Benefit

Some employers offer Supplemental Unemployment Benefits (SUB) plans that top up your EI/QPIP to a higher percentage of your salary — typically 75–95% of gross income for a portion of your leave.

Common Top-Up Structures

  • Federal public service: 93% for 17 weeks (maternity) + 93% for 35 weeks (parental standard)
  • Provincial public service (varies): 85–95% for 15–17 weeks
  • Large banks/telecoms: 75–85% for 15–25 weeks
  • Tech companies (Google, Shopify, etc.): 100% salary for 18–26 weeks (some of the best in Canada)
  • Small businesses: Typically none

How Top-Ups Work

  1. You apply for EI/QPIP and receive your government benefit ($668/week max)
  2. Your employer pays the difference between that and the top-up percentage (e.g., 85% of your salary)
  3. Top-ups are taxable income (just like your regular salary)
  4. Top-ups typically cover only part of your total leave — e.g., 17 weeks of a 52-week leave

If you're job hunting while pregnant or planning pregnancy: Employer top-ups are one of the most valuable benefits to negotiate. A 90% top-up for 25 weeks can be worth $15,000–$30,000+ depending on your salary.

Budgeting Strategies: How to Survive on 55%

Step 1: Calculate Your Real Income Gap (6 Months Before Leave)

  1. Determine your net (after-tax) monthly income while working
  2. Estimate your net monthly EI/QPIP benefit (use the EI calculator)
  3. Calculate the monthly shortfall
  4. Multiply by the number of months on leave

Example (Ontario, $70,000 salary, 12 months leave):

  • Net monthly income (working): ~$4,375
  • Net EI benefit (~$668/week): ~$2,400/month
  • Monthly shortfall: $1,975
  • Total 12-month gap: $23,700

Step 2: Build a Parental Leave Fund

Start saving 6–9 months before your leave. Aim to cover 50–100% of your income gap:

  • Aggressive savers: Cover 100% of the gap ($23,700 in the example above)
  • Moderate savers: Cover 50% of the gap ($11,850) and cut expenses for the rest
  • Minimal savers: Cover 25% ($5,925) and rely on credit/family support (not ideal, but reality for many)

If you save $2,000/month for 6 months, you'll have $12,000 — enough to cover half the gap in our example.

Step 3: Cut Discretionary Spending Before Leave Starts

Practice living on your reduced income 3 months before leave:

  • Cancel unused subscriptions (streaming, gym, apps)
  • Reduce dining out by 50–75%
  • Pause RRSP/TFSA contributions temporarily (controversial, but cash flow matters)
  • Negotiate lower bills (internet, phone, insurance)
  • Defer non-essential purchases (home renos, new car, vacations)

Put the savings into your parental leave fund.

Step 4: Prioritize Essential Spending

During leave, your budget hierarchy should be:

  1. Housing (rent/mortgage): Non-negotiable
  2. Utilities, groceries, transportation: Essential
  3. Minimum debt payments: Protect your credit score
  4. Baby essentials: Diapers, formula (if needed), medical
  5. Everything else: Pause or minimize

Step 5: Maximize the Canada Child Benefit (CCB)

Your CCB starts the month after your child is born. This is tax-free income that offsets new baby costs. (More on this below.)

New Baby Costs: What to Expect in Year One

While your income drops, your expenses increase. Here's the realistic breakdown:

One-Time Setup Costs ($2,500–$6,000)

  • Crib, mattress, changing table: $500–$1,500
  • Stroller: $200–$1,200
  • Car seat: $150–$500
  • Baby monitor: $80–$300
  • Clothing (0–12 months): $300–$800
  • Miscellaneous gear: $500–$1,200 (bottles, breast pump, playmat, swing, bouncer, etc.)

Ways to reduce: Buy secondhand (Facebook Marketplace, Once Upon A Child), accept hand-me-downs, register for a baby shower, use Buy Nothing groups.

Ongoing Monthly Costs ($500–$1,200/month)

  • Diapers: $60–$100/month (disposable) or $25–$50/month (cloth + utilities)
  • Wipes: $15–$25/month
  • Formula (if not breastfeeding): $150–$300/month
  • Childcare (if returning to work mid-leave): $800–$2,000/month (daycare) or $0 if on full leave
  • Medical (non-covered): $50–$150/month (vitamins, ointments, prescriptions, dental once teeth emerge)
  • Clothing (rapid growth): $30–$80/month

Year-one baby costs (excluding childcare): $8,000–$15,000

💡Formula vs Breastfeeding Financial Reality

Formula costs $1,800–$3,600/year. Breastfeeding is "free" but has hidden costs: breast pump ($150–$400), lactation consultant ($100–$500), nursing bras/pads ($100–$200), and significant time investment. Neither choice is financially superior in all cases — choose what works for your family and don't let cost guilt you.

Canada Child Benefit (CCB): Your Monthly Lifeline

The CCB is a tax-free monthly payment for families with children under 18. It's based on your family's net income from the previous tax year.

Maximum CCB (2026)

  • Children under 6: Up to $7,787/year ($649/month)
  • Children 6–17: Up to $6,570/year ($548/month)

CCB Income Thresholds (2026)

  • Family income under $36,502: Full benefit
  • Family income $36,502–$80,000: Gradually reduced
  • Family income above $80,000: Further reduced (but not eliminated)

Example: Family with $85,000 combined income and one child under 6:

  • CCB: ~$5,200/year ($433/month)

Example: Family with $50,000 combined income and one child under 6:

  • CCB: ~$6,900/year ($575/month)

The CCB recalculates every July based on your previous year's tax return. If you or your partner is on parental leave (and earning less), your CCB will increase the following July once your lower income is reflected.

📌CCB Lag: Your First 8 Months

If your baby is born in January 2026, your CCB for the first 8 months (Jan–July 2026) is based on your 2024 tax return (when you were working full income). In July 2026, it recalculates using your 2025 return (which includes your reduced parental leave income) — and your CCB increases. Plan for this lag.

RRSP Strategies During Parental Leave

Parental leave creates a unique tax situation: your income drops significantly, so your marginal tax rate is lower. This has strategic implications for RRSPs.

Should You Contribute to Your RRSP During Leave?

Usually no. RRSP contributions are most valuable when your marginal tax rate is high (30–50%). During parental leave, your marginal rate might be only 20–30%. Save your RRSP contribution room for when you're back at full income and in a higher tax bracket.

Should You Withdraw From Your RRSP During Leave?

Maybe — strategically. If you're in a low tax bracket during leave (e.g., $30,000 total income), withdrawing RRSP funds is taxed at a lower rate than when you contributed. But this is generally only smart if:

  • You're in genuine financial hardship
  • You have no other savings options
  • You understand you're permanently losing that contribution room

The Better Strategy: Use Your TFSA

If you need emergency funds during leave, withdraw from your TFSA instead of your RRSP. TFSA withdrawals are tax-free and you regain the contribution room the following year. RRSPs are taxed on withdrawal and the room is gone forever.

Returning to Work: Childcare Costs and the Break-Even Question

When you return to work, your income goes back up — but so do your expenses, primarily childcare.

Average Canadian Childcare Costs (2026)

  • Infant daycare (under 18 months): $1,200–$2,500/month depending on province
  • Toddler daycare (18+ months): $900–$2,000/month
  • Quebec (subsidized daycare): $9.10/day (~$200/month) — by far the cheapest in Canada
  • Home daycare: $800–$1,500/month
  • Nanny (live-out): $2,000–$3,500/month

The Childcare Expense Deduction (CCED)

You can deduct childcare costs on your tax return:

  • Children under 7: Up to $8,000/year per child
  • Children 7–16: Up to $5,000/year per child
  • Disabled children: Up to $11,000/year

The deduction must be claimed by the lower-income spouse.

If you're paying $18,000/year in daycare for an infant, you can deduct $8,000 — saving you ~$2,400–$4,000 depending on your tax bracket.

Break-Even Analysis: Is It Worth Returning to Work?

Some parents (usually mothers, unfortunately) find that after childcare costs, commuting, and work wardrobe, their net income is minimal. Run the math:

Example: $45,000 salary, one infant in daycare

  • Gross salary: $45,000
  • After-tax income: ~$35,500
  • Childcare cost: $18,000/year (less $3,200 tax deduction benefit) = $14,800 net cost
  • Commuting/parking: $1,800/year
  • Work wardrobe/lunches: $1,200/year
  • Net income after work costs: $17,700/year ($1,475/month)

Is $1,475/month worth it? For some families, yes (career progression, benefits, pension, mental health). For others, staying home longer makes more financial sense — especially if a second child is planned.

Putting It All Together: Your Parental Leave Financial Checklist

6–9 Months Before Leave

  • Calculate your income gap using the EI/QPIP calculator
  • Start saving aggressively — aim for 50–100% of your gap
  • Review your employer benefits (top-up, extended health, life insurance continuation during leave)
  • Open a high-interest savings account for your parental leave fund

3 Months Before Leave

  • Practice living on your reduced budget
  • Buy essential baby gear (used where possible)
  • Apply for EI/QPIP (can be done up to 12 weeks before your due date)
  • Set up direct deposit for EI/QPIP payments

When Baby Arrives

  • Register your baby for the CCB (apply within 12 months of birth — backpay is limited)
  • Register the birth and get a Social Insurance Number for your child
  • Update your employer benefits (add dependent coverage)

During Leave

  • Track your spending weekly (newborn fog makes it easy to lose track)
  • Reassess your budget monthly — adjust as needed
  • File your tax return on time (important for CCB recalculation)
  • If you receive an employer top-up, set aside extra for taxes (it's taxable income)

3 Months Before Returning

  • Research childcare options and get on waitlists (some have 6–12 month waits)
  • Calculate your post-childcare net income
  • Discuss with your partner: staggered return, part-time, extended leave?
