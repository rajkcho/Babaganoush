Parents Helping Kids Buy a First Home — FHSA, Gifting, Co-signing

Rick Minji•March 11, 2026•12 min read

With the average Canadian home price hovering around $680,000 and a 20% down payment requiring $136,000, it's no surprise that many first-time buyers need help from the Bank of Mom and Dad. If you're a parent looking to help your child buy their first home — or a young buyer hoping your parents can help — here's every strategy available in 2026, with the rules, risks, and tax implications you need to know.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • FHSA: Your child can save $8,000/year (up to $40,000 lifetime) with tax-deductible contributions and tax-free withdrawals for a first home. Parents can gift money for contributions.
  • Gifted down payment: No limit on gift amount, no tax on gifts in Canada, but lenders require a signed gift letter confirming no repayment expected
  • Co-signing: Helps your child qualify but puts your credit and finances at serious risk. Consider alternatives first.
  • Home Buyers' Plan (HBP): Withdraw up to $60,000 from RRSP tax-free for a first home (must repay over 15 years)
  • Best approach: Combine FHSA + HBP + parental gift for maximum down payment with minimum tax

FHSA Strategy for Parents + Kids

The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) is the single best tool for first-time home buyers in Canada. Contributions are tax-deductible (like an RRSP) and withdrawals for a qualifying home purchase are completely tax-free (like a TFSA). It's the best of both worlds.

FHSA Rules (Quick Recap)

  • Annual contribution limit: $8,000/year
  • Lifetime limit: $40,000
  • Carry-forward: Up to $8,000 of unused room carries forward (max contribution in any year: $16,000)
  • Eligibility: Canadian resident, 18-71, never owned a home in the current year or past 4 years
  • Time limit: Must use within 15 years of opening (or by age 71)

How Parents Can Help with FHSA

Parents can't contribute directly to their child's FHSA — the account holder must make the contribution. But parents can give their child cash to contribute. Here's the optimal strategy:

  1. Open the FHSA as early as possible. The clock starts ticking when the account is opened — even if your child isn't buying for 5 years, open it now to start accumulating contribution room.
  2. Parents gift $8,000/year in cash. Canada has no gift tax, so this is completely tax-free for both parties.
  3. Child contributes $8,000 to FHSA. They get the tax deduction on their return — potentially saving $2,000-$3,500 in taxes per year depending on their income.
  4. Invest inside the FHSA. Growth is tax-sheltered. Index ETFs (XEQT, VGRO) are common choices.
  5. After 5 years: $40,000 contributed + investment growth = $45,000-$50,000+ for a down payment, completely tax-free.
💡The Tax Deduction Trick

If your child is in a low tax bracket now (student, early career), they can contribute to the FHSA but defer claiming the tax deduction to a future year when their income is higher. This maximizes the value of the deduction. The contribution room is used up, but the deduction can be carried forward indefinitely.

Gifted Down Payments: Rules and Documentation

Canada has no gift tax — you can give your child any amount of money without either party paying tax. However, when that money is used as a down payment, the mortgage lender has strict requirements.

What Lenders Require

  • Gift letter: A signed letter from the parent(s) stating the money is a gift, not a loan, and no repayment is expected
  • Proof of transfer: Bank statements showing the money moving from parent to child
  • 90-day seasoning: Some lenders want to see the gifted funds in the buyer's account for 90 days before closing
  • Source verification: For anti-money laundering compliance, lenders may ask where the parent's money came from (savings, sale of property, etc.)

Gift Letter Template

A proper gift letter should include:

  • Full names and addresses of gift giver(s) and recipient
  • The exact dollar amount of the gift
  • A statement that the funds are a gift with no expectation of repayment
  • The relationship between giver and recipient
  • The property address (if known)
  • Signatures and date
⚠️"Gift" Loans Are Dangerous

Some families plan to call money a "gift" on the letter but expect repayment privately. This is mortgage fraud. If discovered, the mortgage can be called immediately, and you could face criminal charges. If you genuinely expect repayment, explore proper lending options instead. If it's truly a gift, make sure both parties understand that — no strings attached.

Tax Considerations for Parents

  • No gift tax in Canada — the gift itself is tax-free for both giver and receiver
  • If the gift comes from selling investments, the parent pays capital gains tax on the sale — not the child
  • If the parent is liquidating an RRSP to generate the gift, they'll pay income tax on the withdrawal
  • Consider gifting over 2-3 years to spread out any RRSP withdrawals across tax years

Co-signing: Risks and Alternatives

Co-signing means you're equally responsible for the mortgage. If your child misses payments, the lender comes to you. This is not a formality — it's a serious financial commitment.

How Co-signing Affects Parents

  • Your credit: The full mortgage appears on your credit report as a liability
  • Your borrowing capacity: That mortgage debt counts against you if you need a loan, credit line, or your own mortgage
  • Your risk: If your child defaults, you're legally obligated to pay. The lender can sue you, garnish wages, or force the sale of the property.
  • Your relationship: Money and family don't always mix well. What happens if your child divorces and the ex-spouse is on the mortgage?

When Co-signing Might Make Sense

  • Your child has stable income but insufficient credit history (newcomer, recent graduate)
  • Your child can comfortably make the payments — they just need help qualifying
  • You have minimal existing debt and won't need your own borrowing capacity soon
  • You have a clear exit plan (refinance to remove co-signer within 1-3 years as credit improves)

Alternatives to Co-signing

  • Larger gifted down payment: Reduces the mortgage amount so your child qualifies on their own
  • Guarantor instead of co-signer: Some lenders offer guarantor arrangements where you back the mortgage without being on title — slightly less exposure
  • Private lending: Lend your child a formal, documented second mortgage (consult a lawyer)
  • Wait: Sometimes the best strategy is waiting 6-12 months for your child to build more income or credit history
📌The Exit Plan Matters Most

If you do co-sign, agree in writing on a timeline to remove you from the mortgage. Typically, after 1-2 years of payments and credit building, your child can refinance into their own name. Get this in writing — don't assume it'll happen automatically.

Home Buyers' Plan (HBP) from RRSP

The HBP allows first-time home buyers to withdraw up to $60,000 from their RRSP tax-free for a down payment (up from $35,000 as of 2024). For a couple, that's $120,000 combined.

HBP Rules

  • Maximum withdrawal: $60,000 per person
  • Eligibility: First-time buyer (haven't owned in past 4 years)
  • Repayment: Must repay the full amount to your RRSP over 15 years (starting 2 years after withdrawal)
  • Minimum annual repayment: 1/15th of the withdrawn amount per year
  • Miss a repayment? The missed amount is added to your income that year and taxed

HBP Strategy for Parents + Kids

Parents can't withdraw from their own RRSP for their child's home purchase (the HBP is only for your own first home). However:

  • Parents can gift money to the child specifically for RRSP contributions, who then withdraws under the HBP
  • The child gets a tax deduction on the RRSP contribution, then a tax-free HBP withdrawal
  • Important: RRSP contributions used for HBP must have been in the account for at least 90 days before withdrawal
⚠️The HBP Repayment Trap

Many first-time buyers withdraw from their RRSP via HBP and then forget to repay or can't afford to. Missed repayments are taxed as income. If you withdraw $60,000 and miss all repayments, you'll owe tax on $4,000/year for 15 years — that could be $1,200-$2,000 in extra tax annually. Make sure the repayment plan is realistic before withdrawing.

The Optimal Combined Strategy

For maximum down payment with minimum tax, combine all available tools:

Example: Buying a $600,000 Home (20% down = $120,000)

  1. FHSA (5 years of contributions): $40,000 + ~$8,000 growth = ~$48,000 (tax-free withdrawal)
  2. HBP from RRSP: $60,000 (tax-free, must repay over 15 years)
  3. Parental gift: $12,000 (gift letter, no tax implications)
  4. Total down payment: $120,000 (20%) — no CMHC insurance needed

Tax Benefits Along the Way

  • FHSA contributions: $40,000 in deductions → ~$12,000-$16,000 in tax savings over 5 years
  • RRSP contributions (before HBP): Additional tax deductions
  • No tax on FHSA withdrawal or HBP withdrawal
  • No tax on the gift
💡Start the FHSA Clock Now

Even if your child is 18 and not planning to buy for 8 years, open the FHSA immediately. Contribution room starts accumulating from the day you open it. An 18-year-old who opens an FHSA now can have the full $40,000 room available by 23, giving them maximum flexibility. The account can stay open for 15 years, so there's no rush to use it — but you want the room available when they're ready.

The Bottom Line

  • FHSA is the #1 tool. Open one for your child ASAP — even with a small initial contribution. The combination of tax deduction + tax-free growth + tax-free withdrawal is unmatched.
  • Gifting is simple and tax-free. Just follow the lender's gift letter requirements and plan the timing.
  • Co-signing is a last resort. Explore every alternative before putting your credit and finances on the line.
  • HBP is powerful but comes with strings. The 15-year repayment obligation is real — make sure your child can handle it.
  • Combine strategies for maximum impact. FHSA + HBP + gift can easily reach 20% down on a median-priced home.
  • Get professional advice. A mortgage broker and tax accountant can optimize the strategy for your specific family situation.

🎯 Parent-Child Home Buying Checklist

  • ✅ Open child's FHSA as early as possible (even at 18)
  • ✅ Gift $8,000/year for FHSA contributions
  • ✅ Ensure RRSP contributions sit for 90+ days before HBP withdrawal
  • ✅ Prepare a proper gift letter for lender documentation
  • ✅ Transfer gifted funds 90 days before closing if possible
  • ✅ Avoid co-signing if a larger gift can solve the qualifying issue
  • ✅ Plan the HBP repayment schedule before withdrawing
