The Smith Manoeuvre Explained: Making Your Mortgage Tax-Deductible

Rick Minji•April 11, 2026•14 min read

Americans can deduct their mortgage interest. Canadians can't. It's one of the most frustrating asymmetries in North American tax law, and it costs Canadian homeowners thousands of dollars a year in after-tax carrying costs. But there's a perfectly legal strategy — named after financial planner Fraser Smith — that effectively converts your non-deductible mortgage into a tax-deductible investment loan.

It's called the Smith Manoeuvre, and it's been used by financially savvy Canadians for over two decades. When executed properly, it can save you tens of thousands in taxes over the life of your mortgage while simultaneously building an investment portfolio. When executed poorly — or by someone who shouldn't be doing it — it can amplify losses and create real financial pain.

Let's break down exactly how it works, who it's for, and the risks nobody should ignore.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • The Smith Manoeuvre converts non-deductible mortgage debt into tax-deductible investment debt using a readvanceable mortgage (HELOC that grows as you pay down principal)
  • You need a readvanceable mortgage — not all lenders offer them. Manulife One, National Bank, and some credit unions are popular choices
  • The CRA allows interest deduction on borrowed money used to earn investment income (dividends, interest, or rent) — this is the legal foundation
  • Over a 25-year mortgage, the Smith Manoeuvre can save $50,000–$150,000+ in taxes depending on your mortgage size, rate, and marginal tax rate
  • The strategy increases your total debt exposure and requires discipline. If markets crash, you still owe the HELOC balance
  • This is not a beginner strategy — get professional tax and financial advice before implementing

What Is the Smith Manoeuvre?

At its core, the Smith Manoeuvre exploits a simple distinction in Canadian tax law:

  • Mortgage interest on your home = NOT tax-deductible
  • Interest on money borrowed to invest (to earn income) = tax-deductible

The strategy works by gradually replacing your non-deductible mortgage with a deductible investment loan. Every time you make a mortgage payment, a portion goes to principal. That freed-up equity is immediately re-borrowed through a HELOC (Home Equity Line of Credit) and invested in income-producing assets. The HELOC interest becomes tax-deductible because the borrowed funds are being used to earn investment income.

Your total debt stays roughly the same — but the type of debt shifts from "bad" (non-deductible) to "good" (deductible). By the time your mortgage is paid off, you have a fully tax-deductible HELOC and an investment portfolio that's been compounding for years.

📌The Name Behind the Strategy

Fraser Smith, a Vancouver financial planner, popularized this strategy in his 2002 book The Smith Manoeuvre. He didn't invent the underlying tax rules — the deductibility of investment loan interest has been in the Income Tax Act for decades. He simply packaged the concept into a repeatable, systematic strategy that ordinary homeowners could follow. The updated edition, Master Your Mortgage for Financial Freedom (2019), covers the latest variations.

Why It Works: The Tax Law Foundation

The Canada Revenue Agency allows you to deduct interest on borrowed money under paragraph 20(1)(c) of the Income Tax Act, provided the money is used to earn income from a business or property. "Income from property" includes dividends, interest, and rental income.

The Key CRA Requirements

  1. Direct tracing: The borrowed funds must be directly used for income-earning purposes. You can't borrow, deposit into a personal account, pay some bills, and then invest the remainder. The money must flow directly from the HELOC to the investment account.
  2. Income-earning purpose: The investments must have a reasonable expectation of earning income (dividends, interest, rent). Pure capital gains plays (like buying gold or non-dividend-paying growth stocks) are riskier from a deductibility standpoint.
  3. Ongoing deductibility: Even if your investments lose value, the interest remains deductible as long as the original purpose was to earn income. The Supreme Court of Canada confirmed this in Ludco Enterprises v. Canada (2001).
⚠️Never Invest in Registered Accounts

Borrowed money invested in a TFSA, RRSP, or RESP does NOT qualify for the interest deduction. These are tax-sheltered accounts, not "income-earning" investments in the CRA's eyes. Smith Manoeuvre investments must go into a non-registered (taxable) account. This is a common mistake that can void the entire deduction.

Step-by-Step Mechanics

What You Need

  1. A readvanceable mortgage: This is a mortgage product where the HELOC limit automatically increases as you pay down mortgage principal. Not all mortgages offer this — it's the single most important requirement.
  2. A non-registered investment account: With a brokerage that allows you to receive direct deposits from your HELOC.
  3. Discipline and record-keeping: You need to track every HELOC withdrawal and its corresponding investment purchase for CRA compliance.

The Monthly Cycle

Here's what happens each month:

  1. Make your regular mortgage payment. Let's say $2,500 goes to your mortgage. Of that, $800 is principal and $1,700 is interest.
  2. Your HELOC limit increases by $800 (the principal you just paid down). This happens automatically with a readvanceable mortgage.
  3. Borrow the $800 from your HELOC. Transfer it directly to your non-registered investment account.
  4. Invest the $800 in dividend-paying stocks, Canadian equity ETFs, or other income-producing assets.
  5. Deduct the HELOC interest on your tax return as an investment expense (Line 22100).

Repeat every month for the life of your mortgage. Over time, your mortgage balance shrinks to zero while your HELOC balance grows to equal what your original mortgage was — but now all the interest is tax-deductible.

Worked Example: $500,000 Mortgage

Let's run the numbers on a typical scenario:

  • Mortgage: $500,000 at 4.5%, 25-year amortization
  • Monthly payment: ~$2,756
  • HELOC rate: Prime + 0.5% (assume 5.5%)
  • Marginal tax rate: 43.41% (Ontario, $100K–$150K income)
  • Investment return: 7% average (Canadian dividend ETFs)

Year 1

  • Principal paid down: ~$10,600
  • HELOC balance (invested): $10,600
  • HELOC interest paid: ~$290
  • Tax deduction value: $290 × 43.41% = $126 saved
  • Investment portfolio value: ~$11,000 (with returns)

Year 10

  • HELOC balance (invested): ~$138,000
  • Annual HELOC interest: ~$7,600
  • Tax deduction value: $7,600 × 43.41% = $3,300 saved per year
  • Investment portfolio value: ~$195,000 (with reinvested returns)

Year 25 (Mortgage Fully Paid Off)

  • HELOC balance: ~$500,000 (now fully tax-deductible)
  • Annual HELOC interest: ~$27,500
  • Tax deduction value: $27,500 × 43.41% = $11,940 saved per year
  • Investment portfolio value: ~$710,000–$780,000
  • Net position: Portfolio ($750K) minus HELOC ($500K) = ~$250K net wealth created
  • Total tax savings over 25 years: approximately $95,000–$120,000
💡The Tax Refund Accelerator

The "Cash Flow Dam" variation puts your tax refund back onto the mortgage as a lump-sum prepayment, which frees up even more HELOC room to invest. This accelerates the entire process — potentially shaving 5–7 years off your mortgage and adding tens of thousands more to your portfolio. We cover this in the Variations section below.

Plain Jane vs Accelerator Variations

Plain Jane Smith Manoeuvre

The basic version described above. Every month, you reborrow the principal portion and invest it. Simple, systematic, and effective. This is where most people should start.

The Cash Flow Dam

Instead of paying business or investment expenses from your chequing account, you pay them from the HELOC. Then you use the freed-up cash to make extra mortgage payments. This converts personal (non-deductible) spending into deductible HELOC debt while accelerating mortgage paydown. Works best for self-employed individuals or those with significant investment expenses.

The Debt Swap

If you have other non-deductible debts (car loans, credit card balances), you can use HELOC funds to pay them off — but only if you immediately redirect equivalent cash flow to investments. The direct tracing rules are strict here. Consult a tax professional before attempting this.

The Guerrilla Capitalization

Rather than investing the borrowed HELOC funds and then separately paying HELOC interest from your paycheque, you capitalize the HELOC interest — paying the HELOC interest from the HELOC itself. The CRA has accepted this approach (IT-533), but it's aggressive and creates compound deductible debt. The math works in your favour, but the risk is higher.

⚠️Keep It Clean

The biggest compliance risk is contaminating your HELOC. If you use the same HELOC for both Smith Manoeuvre investing and personal expenses (renovations, vacations, etc.), you create a mixed-use loan that's a nightmare to deduct properly. Best practice: have a dedicated HELOC sub-account used exclusively for Smith Manoeuvre investments. Many readvanceable mortgages allow multiple HELOC segments.

Lender & Mortgage Requirements

Not every mortgage works for the Smith Manoeuvre. You specifically need a readvanceable mortgage — one where your HELOC credit limit automatically increases as your mortgage principal decreases. Here are the main options in Canada:

Lenders That Offer Readvanceable Mortgages

  • Manulife One: All-in-one account that combines mortgage, HELOC, chequing, and savings. Very Smith Manoeuvre-friendly, though the rate is typically prime (no discount).
  • National Bank All-In-One: Similar to Manulife One. Competitive rates and flexible sub-accounts.
  • TD Home Equity FlexLine: Readvanceable with multiple segments. One of the most popular choices.
  • Scotia STEP (Scotia Total Equity Plan): Allows up to 4 mortgage segments and revolving credit segments.
  • RBC Homeline Plan: Combines mortgage with a readvanceable line of credit.
  • BMO ReadiLine: Readvanceable, though terms vary.
  • MCAP, First National, many credit unions: Various readvanceable products available through brokers.

What to Watch For

  • Maximum LTV: Most readvanceable mortgages cap the combined mortgage + HELOC at 80% of your home's value. The HELOC portion alone is typically capped at 65% LTV.
  • Rate premiums: Some all-in-one products charge slightly higher rates. Calculate whether the Smith Manoeuvre tax savings offset any rate premium.
  • Sub-account flexibility: You want the ability to create a separate HELOC sub-account dedicated to Smith Manoeuvre investing. This keeps your records clean for the CRA.
  • Prepayment privileges: Better prepayment options accelerate the strategy. Look for at least 15–20% annual lump-sum privileges.

What to Invest In

The CRA requires that borrowed funds be invested with a reasonable expectation of earning income. This means you should focus on income-producing investments in a non-registered account:

Strong Choices

  • Canadian dividend ETFs (e.g., XDV, VDY, CDZ): Eligible Canadian dividends get the dividend tax credit, making them tax-efficient even in a non-registered account
  • Canadian blue-chip dividend stocks (banks, telecoms, utilities, pipelines): Steady income, long track records
  • Broad Canadian equity ETFs (XIU, XIC, VCN): Some dividend yield plus growth exposure
  • REITs (in moderation): High income yield, though distributions are taxed less favourably

Acceptable but Less Ideal

  • US or international dividend ETFs: Foreign dividends don't get the Canadian dividend tax credit and are taxed as regular income. Still deductible, but less tax-efficient.
  • Balanced ETFs (VBAL, XBAL): Interest and dividend components provide income. The fixed-income portion generates interest that's fully taxable.

Avoid

  • Non-dividend growth stocks exclusively: While capital gains may eventually result, the CRA could challenge the "income-earning purpose" if there's no reasonable dividend or interest expectation
  • Speculative investments: Crypto, meme stocks, penny stocks — the "income-earning purpose" test is harder to defend
  • Registered accounts: TFSA, RRSP, RESP — interest is NOT deductible on borrowed money invested here
💡The Canadian Dividend Advantage

Canadian eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% but then receive a federal dividend tax credit of 15.02% plus provincial credits. For someone in the 43% marginal bracket in Ontario, the effective tax rate on eligible dividends is only about 25% — compared to 43% on interest income or foreign dividends. Pairing the Smith Manoeuvre with Canadian dividend stocks is the most tax-efficient combination.

The Risks — Seriously, Read This

The Smith Manoeuvre is often presented as a "free money" strategy. It's not. Here are the real risks:

1. Market Risk

You're borrowing against your home to invest. If markets decline significantly (2008, 2020, 2022), your portfolio drops in value but your HELOC balance remains the same. In a worst-case scenario, you could owe more on your HELOC than your investments are worth. Over a 25-year period, markets have historically recovered — but "historically" isn't a guarantee, and living through a 40% drawdown while owing $300K on a HELOC tests even strong stomachs.

2. Interest Rate Risk

HELOCs are variable-rate. If prime jumps from 5% to 8%, your carrying costs spike dramatically. On a $300K HELOC, that's an extra $9,000/year in interest. Yes, it's still deductible — but you need the cash flow to service it.

3. Sequence of Returns Risk

If markets crash early in your Smith Manoeuvre journey, you have less time and less capital to recover. The strategy works best when you get average or above-average returns in the early years — something you can't control.

4. Discipline Risk

The Smith Manoeuvre requires never using your investment HELOC for personal expenses. One slip — using the HELOC to renovate your kitchen — contaminates the account and creates a mixed-use loan that's far harder to deduct. Many people start with good intentions and break the rules years later.

5. CRA Audit Risk

While the strategy is legal, the CRA does audit investment interest deductions. You need to maintain clear documentation: HELOC statements showing direct transfers to your investment account, brokerage statements showing purchases with those exact amounts, and records showing the income-earning nature of your investments.

6. Liquidity Risk

If you need to sell investments during a downturn to cover expenses, you lock in losses but still owe the HELOC. The strategy assumes you can hold investments for the long term without being forced to sell.

⚠️The Leverage Warning

The Smith Manoeuvre is leveraged investing. Full stop. Leverage amplifies gains AND losses. If your investments return 7% and your HELOC costs 5.5%, you profit. If your investments return -15% and your HELOC costs 5.5%, you lose on both sides. Never implement the Smith Manoeuvre with money you can't afford to lose, and never do it if the HELOC payments would strain your monthly budget.

CRA Rules & Compliance

What to Claim

  • Line 22100 (Carrying charges and interest expenses): Deduct the HELOC interest attributable to your Smith Manoeuvre investment account
  • T5 slips: Report all investment income (dividends, interest, capital gains) from your non-registered Smith Manoeuvre portfolio
  • Supporting documents: Keep HELOC statements, brokerage trade confirmations, and a clear paper trail for at least 7 years

Common Pitfalls

  • Mixed-use HELOC: If you borrow $50K for investing and $20K for a car from the same HELOC, you can only deduct interest on the $50K portion. Better to use separate sub-accounts.
  • Investing in registered accounts: Borrowed money → RRSP/TFSA = no deduction. Period.
  • Return of capital distributions: Some ETFs pay return of capital (ROC), which reduces your adjusted cost base. ROC doesn't count as "income" for the income-earning purpose test, but as long as the investment has a reasonable expectation of earning dividends or interest overall, you're fine.
  • Selling investments and not reinvesting: If you sell a Smith Manoeuvre investment and pocket the cash, the HELOC interest on that portion is no longer deductible. You must reinvest the proceeds or pay down the HELOC.

The Singleton Shuffle

What if you already have a non-readvanceable mortgage and non-registered investments? The Singleton Shuffle (named after the Singleton v. Canada Supreme Court case) allows you to sell existing investments, use the proceeds to pay down your mortgage, then borrow from a HELOC to repurchase the investments. The new HELOC interest is now deductible. This works because the CRA traces the current use of borrowed funds, not the historical chain.

Consult a tax professional before attempting this — the tracing rules are strict and you may trigger capital gains on the sale.

Who Should (and Shouldn't) Do This

Good Candidates ✅

  • Stable income earners with a marginal tax rate of 40%+ (the higher your rate, the more valuable the deduction)
  • Long time horizon: At least 10–15 years remaining on your mortgage
  • Strong cash flow: Can comfortably service both mortgage and HELOC payments even if rates rise
  • Emotionally steady: Won't panic-sell during a market crash while owing $200K on a HELOC
  • Good record-keepers: Can maintain clean documentation for CRA compliance
  • Already maxed out registered accounts: TFSA and RRSP should be full before leveraging in non-registered

Poor Candidates ❌

  • Low marginal tax rate (under 30%): The deduction isn't worth the risk and complexity
  • Tight cash flow: If a rate increase of 2% would stress your budget, leverage is dangerous
  • Short time horizon: Planning to sell your home in 3–5 years — not enough time for the strategy to compound
  • Unfilled registered accounts: Max your TFSA and RRSP first. Tax-free and tax-deferred growth in registered accounts beats leveraged non-registered investing for most people.
  • Emotional investors: If you sold everything in March 2020 or December 2018, leveraged investing will magnify that tendency
  • Variable or uncertain income: Contract workers, seasonal employees, or anyone without stable employment
📌The Priority Stack

Before considering the Smith Manoeuvre, make sure you've done these first: (1) Emergency fund of 3–6 months, (2) High-interest debt paid off, (3) TFSA maxed, (4) RRSP contributed to optimal level, (5) FHSA maxed (if applicable). The Smith Manoeuvre is an advanced optimization, not a foundational strategy.

The Bottom Line

The Smith Manoeuvre is one of the most powerful — and most misunderstood — tax strategies available to Canadian homeowners. It's not a loophole, it's not a scam, and it's not free money. It's a disciplined, long-term approach to converting dead-weight mortgage interest into productive, tax-deductible investment capital.

When it works, the results are compelling: six-figure tax savings, a substantial investment portfolio built alongside your home equity, and a mortgage that effectively pays for itself through tax refunds. When it doesn't work — because of poor market timing, undisciplined execution, or inadequate cash flow — it amplifies financial stress.

  • If you're a high-income earner with a stable job, maxed registered accounts, strong cash flow, and a long time horizon — the Smith Manoeuvre deserves serious consideration
  • If any of the risk factors above apply — focus on the basics first. There's nothing wrong with a boring, fully-paid-off mortgage
  • Either way, get professional advice. A tax accountant and financial planner who understand the Smith Manoeuvre are worth every dollar of their fees
