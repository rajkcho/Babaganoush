How Much House Can You Actually Afford in Canada?

Rick Minji•March 25, 2026•14 min read

Every online mortgage calculator will tell you the maximum you qualify for. Banks will pre-approve you for an amount that makes your stomach flip. And your real estate agent will show you homes at the very top of your budget. None of these people have to make your mortgage payments.

The question isn't "how much will the bank lend me?" — it's "how much can I comfortably pay every month without sacrificing everything else I care about?" Those are two very different numbers, and the gap between them is where financial stress lives.

In 2026, with average home prices ranging from $350,000 in parts of Atlantic Canada to $1.1M+ in Toronto and $1.3M+ in Vancouver, getting the affordability math right has never mattered more. Let's break down exactly how lenders calculate what you qualify for, what you should actually spend, and what it realistically takes to buy in different Canadian cities.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • Banks will approve you for more than you should borrow. Maximum qualification ≠ comfortable affordability
  • Lenders use GDS (≤39%) and TDS (≤44%) ratios — but comfortable living usually means keeping total housing costs under 30% of gross income
  • The stress test qualifies you at your contract rate + 2%, or 5.25% — whichever is higher
  • Down payment minimums: 5% on first $500K, 10% on $500K–$1.5M, 20% on $1.5M+
  • Budget an extra $300–$800/month beyond your mortgage for property tax, insurance, maintenance, and utilities
  • A household earning $100K can comfortably afford a home in the $400K–$475K range — far below what the bank will approve

What the Bank Says vs What You Can Actually Handle

Here's the uncomfortable truth: banks are in the business of lending money. Their affordability calculations are designed to determine the maximum risk they're willing to take, not the maximum comfort you should aim for. A bank will approve you based on your gross income — the number on your T4 before taxes, CPP, EI, and benefits are deducted.

But you don't pay your mortgage with gross income. You pay it with what hits your bank account, which is typically 25–35% less than your gross depending on your province and tax bracket.

The Gap in Practice

Consider a household earning $120,000 gross in Ontario:

  • Gross monthly income: $10,000
  • Net monthly income (after tax, CPP, EI): ~$7,400
  • Maximum bank-approved housing costs (39% GDS): $3,900/month
  • That $3,900 as a percentage of net income: 53%

Spending 53% of your take-home pay on housing leaves $3,500 for literally everything else — groceries, transportation, childcare, insurance, savings, entertainment, and emergencies. For a family, that's tight. For a family in a city with expensive childcare, it's borderline impossible.

⚠️The 30% Rule — Still Relevant

The old guideline of spending no more than 30% of gross income on housing exists for a reason. At 30%, you still have room for savings, retirement contributions, and unexpected costs. At 39% (the bank's GDS maximum), you're house-rich and everything-else-poor. Aim for 30% of gross — or better yet, 30% of net.

GDS and TDS Ratios: How Lenders Do the Math

Every Canadian mortgage lender uses two ratios to determine how much you qualify for. Understanding these is essential — not so you can maximize them, but so you know exactly what you're being measured against.

Gross Debt Service (GDS) Ratio — Maximum 39%

GDS measures housing costs as a percentage of gross income. "Housing costs" includes:

  • Mortgage payment (principal + interest, calculated at the stress test rate)
  • Property taxes (annual ÷ 12)
  • Heating costs (estimated, typically $100–$175/month)
  • 50% of condo fees (if applicable)

Formula: GDS = (Mortgage + Property Tax + Heat + 50% Condo Fees) ÷ Gross Income × 100

Total Debt Service (TDS) Ratio — Maximum 44%

TDS adds all your other debt obligations to the GDS calculation:

  • Everything in GDS, plus:
  • Car payments
  • Student loan payments
  • Credit card minimum payments (typically 3% of balance)
  • Lines of credit payments
  • Other loan obligations

Formula: TDS = (All Housing Costs + All Debt Payments) ÷ Gross Income × 100

📌Insured vs Uninsured Mortgages

If your down payment is less than 20%, you need CMHC mortgage insurance. Insured mortgages have stricter ratio limits: GDS ≤ 39% and TDS ≤ 44%. Some uninsured lenders (20%+ down payment) may stretch to GDS 42% / TDS 47% for strong applicants — but that doesn't mean you should aim for it.

Worked Example

Let's say a couple earns $150,000 combined gross ($12,500/month) and wants to buy a $650,000 home with 10% down ($65,000):

  • Mortgage amount: $585,000 + CMHC insurance (~$22,750) = $607,750
  • Stress test rate: 6.64% (contract rate 4.64% + 2%)
  • Monthly mortgage at stress test rate (25-yr am): ~$4,130
  • Property tax: $420/month
  • Heating: $150/month
  • GDS: ($4,130 + $420 + $150) ÷ $12,500 = 37.6% ✅ Under 39%

They qualify. But at their actual contract rate (4.64%), the mortgage payment is ~$3,430/month. Add property tax, heat, insurance, and maintenance, and they're looking at roughly $4,500/month in total housing costs — 36% of gross, or about 48% of net income. That's a stretch.

The Mortgage Stress Test: What It Is and Why It Exists

Since 2018, every federally regulated mortgage in Canada — whether insured or uninsured — must pass the mortgage stress test. You qualify at the higher of:

  • Your actual contract rate + 2 percentage points, or
  • The Bank of Canada's mortgage qualifying rate (currently 5.25%)

In early 2026, with typical 5-year fixed rates around 4.4–4.8%, the stress test rate is approximately 6.4–6.8%. This is the rate used to calculate your GDS and TDS ratios for qualification purposes.

What the Stress Test Means in Practice

The stress test reduces your maximum purchasing power by roughly 18–22% compared to qualifying at the actual contract rate. A household that could afford an $800,000 home at 4.6% can only qualify for about $640,000–$660,000 after the stress test.

This feels frustrating — but the stress test exists because interest rates change. If you locked in at 4.6% and rates are 6.6% when you renew in 5 years, you need to be able to handle those payments. The stress test ensures you can.

💡Variable Rate Stress Test

If you choose a variable rate mortgage, you're still stress-tested at the variable rate + 2% (or 5.25%, whichever is higher). The advantage of variable isn't qualification — it's potentially lower payments during your term. But your maximum purchase price will be similar regardless of which rate type you choose.

Down Payment Rules in Canada

Canada has a tiered down payment system based on the purchase price:

  • First $500,000: Minimum 5% down
  • $500,001 to $1,499,999: 5% on first $500K + 10% on the remainder
  • $1,500,000+: Minimum 20% down (no mortgage insurance available)

📌CMHC Insurance Adds to Your Mortgage

With less than 20% down, CMHC mortgage insurance (2.8–4.0% of the mortgage amount) gets added to your mortgage. On a $600K home with minimum down, that's roughly $22,600 added to your loan — and you pay interest on it for 25 years. This is one reason why saving for a larger down payment can save you tens of thousands over the life of the mortgage.

Where to Get Your Down Payment

  • FHSA (First Home Savings Account): Up to $40,000 lifetime ($8,000/year), tax-deductible contributions, tax-free withdrawals for a home purchase
  • RRSP Home Buyers' Plan: Withdraw up to $60,000 ($120,000 per couple) interest-free — must repay over 15 years
  • TFSA: Withdraw any amount, any time, tax-free — the most flexible option
  • Gifted down payment: Accepted by all lenders with a signed gift letter confirming no repayment required
  • Personal savings: The tried-and-true approach

The Hidden Costs That Blow Up Your Budget

Your mortgage payment is just the beginning. Here's what most first-time buyers underestimate:

Closing Costs (Budget 1.5–4% of Purchase Price)

  • Land transfer tax: Varies by province. In Ontario, a $600K home costs ~$8,475 in provincial LTT (first-time buyers get up to $4,000 rebate). Toronto adds a municipal LTT of another ~$8,475.
  • Legal fees: $1,200–$2,500 for a real estate lawyer
  • Home inspection: $400–$600
  • Title insurance: $250–$500
  • Appraisal fee: $300–$500 (sometimes covered by lender)
  • Moving costs: $1,000–$3,000+

Ongoing Monthly Costs Beyond the Mortgage

  • Property tax: $200–$600+/month depending on municipality and home value
  • Home insurance: $100–$250/month
  • Utilities (hydro, gas, water): $200–$400/month
  • Maintenance reserve: Budget 1% of home value annually ($400–$800+/month for a $500K–$1M home)
  • Condo fees: $300–$800+/month if applicable (covers some of the above)
⚠️The Maintenance Trap

New homeowners consistently underestimate maintenance costs. The roof needs replacing every 20–25 years ($8,000–$15,000). The furnace lasts 15–20 years ($4,000–$7,000). Appliances die. Pipes leak. Windows fog. Budget 1% of your home's value per year for maintenance — on a $600K home, that's $6,000/year or $500/month. Don't skip this.

The Lifestyle Budget Test (What Banks Don't Check)

Lenders don't look at your Netflix subscription, your kids' activities, your grocery bill, or your retirement savings. They only check income and existing debt payments. But you need to eat, and you probably want to retire someday. Here's a more honest affordability test:

Step 1: Calculate Your True Monthly Take-Home

After income tax, CPP, EI, and any payroll deductions (benefits, pension, parking). This is the actual money you have to work with.

Step 2: List Your Non-Housing Fixed Costs

  • Groceries & household supplies
  • Transportation (car payments, insurance, gas, transit)
  • Childcare or child-related costs
  • Insurance (life, disability, health)
  • Phone and internet
  • Minimum debt payments
  • Regular subscriptions

Step 3: Set Aside Savings First

  • Emergency fund contribution: At least 5% of net income
  • Retirement savings: At least 10% of gross income (RRSP/TFSA)
  • Other savings goals: Vacation, kids' activities, RESP

Step 4: What's Left = Your Housing Budget

Take-home income − non-housing expenses − savings = maximum monthly housing costs (mortgage + property tax + insurance + utilities + maintenance)

This bottom-up approach almost always produces a number lower than what the bank approves you for. That lower number is your real affordability ceiling.

💡The Rent Test

If you're currently renting, here's a quick sanity check: your total housing costs as an owner (mortgage + tax + insurance + utilities + maintenance) will typically be 30–60% higher than your current rent for an equivalent property. If you're comfortable spending $2,000/month on rent, don't assume you can handle a $2,000/month mortgage — because the mortgage is only part of the total cost.

When Stretching Your Budget Makes Sense (And When It Doesn't)

It Might Be Worth Stretching If:

  • Your income is reliably growing — early-career professionals with clear salary trajectory may grow into their payments within 2–3 years
  • You're buying in a high-appreciation market — if the home is likely to build equity, short-term tightness can pay off
  • You have a large emergency fund (6+ months) to cushion any disruptions
  • Your non-housing expenses are genuinely low — no car payment, no childcare, minimal debt
  • You have rental income potential — a basement suite or income property component

Don't Stretch If:

  • You're already carrying significant debt — car loans, student loans, or credit card balances
  • Your income is variable — commission-based, contract, or self-employed without 2+ years of stable earnings
  • You have no emergency fund — one job loss or major repair could cascade into mortgage default
  • You're sacrificing retirement savings — skipping RRSP/TFSA contributions to afford the mortgage means trading your future for your present
  • Both partners must work full-time to qualify — no room for parental leave, career changes, or health issues

Your Action Plan: From Browsing to Buying

6–12 Months Before Buying

  1. Run the lifestyle budget test above to determine your true affordability ceiling
  2. Check your credit score — aim for 680+ for the best rates; 740+ for tier-1 pricing
  3. Pay down high-interest debt — every $300/month in debt payments reduces your purchasing power by ~$50,000
  4. Max out your FHSA ($8,000/year) and consider the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan
  5. Save for closing costs separately from your down payment — budget 1.5–4% of purchase price

3–6 Months Before Buying

  1. Get a mortgage pre-approval — this locks in a rate for 90–120 days and shows sellers you're serious
  2. Talk to a mortgage broker, not just your bank. Brokers access 30+ lenders and often find better rates
  3. Set your personal maximum below the pre-approved amount — write it down and commit to it
  4. Research neighbourhoods — factor in commute costs, school quality, and future development plans

When You Find a Home

  1. Don't waive the inspection — a $500 inspection can save you $50,000 in hidden problems
  2. Include financing and inspection conditions in your offer unless the market absolutely demands otherwise
  3. Have a real estate lawyer review everything before you sign — this is non-negotiable
  4. Stress-test yourself: can you handle payments if rates rise 2% at renewal? If one partner loses their income for 6 months?

The Bottom Line

How much house you can afford in Canada comes down to three numbers: what the bank will approve, what you can comfortably pay, and what's available in your market. The bank's number is usually the highest. Your comfortable number should be the one you actually use. And the market will determine whether those numbers buy you a detached home, a townhouse, or a condo.

The most important thing you can do is be honest with yourself about your total monthly costs — not just the mortgage payment. Property tax, insurance, utilities, and maintenance add $300–$800/month on top of your mortgage. Factor those in before you fall in love with a listing.

And remember: your first home doesn't have to be your forever home. Buy what you can comfortably afford today, build equity, and upgrade when your income and equity allow it. The worst outcome isn't buying a smaller home — it's buying a bigger one and spending the next five years stressed about making payments.
