Should You Incorporate as a Freelancer in Canada?

Rick Minji•March 29, 2026•14 min read

Every freelancer in Canada eventually asks the same question: should I incorporate? Your accountant probably said "it depends." Your friend who incorporated swears it saves them thousands. The internet is full of conflicting advice. Here's the actual math — because whether incorporation makes sense comes down to numbers, not opinions.

The short answer: if you're consistently earning over $80,000–$100,000 in net self-employment income and can leave money inside the corporation, incorporation likely makes sense. Below that, the costs and complexity usually outweigh the benefits. But the long answer involves tax integration, salary vs dividend decisions, liability protection, and a few traps that can turn your "tax savings" into a CRA headache.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • A sole proprietorship is simpler and cheaper — income is taxed on your personal return at marginal rates (up to ~53% depending on province)
  • A Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC) pays only 12.2% combined tax (Ontario) on the first $500K of active business income thanks to the small business deduction
  • The real benefit is tax deferral — money left inside the corp grows at a much lower initial tax rate
  • Once you pay yourself salary or dividends, the tax integration system is designed to produce roughly the same total tax as if you'd earned the income personally
  • Incorporation costs $2,000–$5,000+ per year in accounting, legal, and maintenance fees
  • Watch out for Personal Services Business (PSB) rules — if CRA reclassifies your corp, you lose the small business deduction entirely

Sole Proprietorship vs Corporation: The Basics

Before diving into tax math, let's clarify what these structures actually are.

Sole Proprietorship

  • What it is: You and the business are the same legal entity. There's no separation.
  • Registration: Register a business name with your province (~$60–$80 in Ontario). That's it.
  • Taxation: All business income flows directly to your personal tax return (T2125). You pay personal tax rates.
  • Liability: You're personally liable for all business debts and obligations.
  • CPP: You pay both the employee and employer portions of CPP (~$7,700 total for 2026 on maximum pensionable earnings).
  • Accounting: Simple bookkeeping. No separate corporate tax return required.

Corporation (CCPC)

  • What it is: A separate legal entity. The corporation earns income, pays tax, and then distributes to you via salary or dividends.
  • Registration: Federal ($200 online via Corporations Canada) or provincial ($300+ in Ontario). Plus articles of incorporation, bylaws, minute book.
  • Taxation: The corporation files its own T2 return. You file a personal return for whatever you take out as salary/dividends.
  • Liability: Limited to the corporation's assets (with significant exceptions — see below).
  • CPP: If you pay yourself a salary, the corp pays the employer portion and you pay the employee portion. If dividends only, no CPP.
  • Accounting: Separate books, corporate tax filings, annual returns, minute book maintenance.
📌What About an LLC?

LLCs don't exist in Canada (they're a US structure). The Canadian equivalent is a corporation. Some provinces have Limited Liability Partnerships (LLPs), but these are mainly for professional firms (lawyers, accountants). If you're a solo freelancer, your options are sole proprietorship, partnership, or corporation.

The Tax Math: Where the Savings Actually Come From

Here's the core concept that confuses most people: incorporation doesn't eliminate tax — it defers it. Canada's tax system is designed around "integration," meaning the total tax you pay on business income should theoretically be the same whether earned personally or through a corporation. In practice, there are gaps — and those gaps are where the opportunity lies.

How It Works (Ontario Example, 2026)

Sole Proprietor earning $150,000 net:

  • Federal + Ontario personal tax: approximately $38,500
  • CPP (both portions): approximately $7,700
  • Total tax burden: ~$46,200
  • Cash in your pocket: ~$103,800

Corporation earning $150,000, paying you $75,000 salary:

  • Corporate tax on $75,000 retained profit (12.2% in Ontario): $9,150
  • Your personal tax on $75,000 salary: approximately $14,800
  • CPP on salary (both portions): approximately $6,400
  • Total tax (this year): ~$30,350
  • Cash in your pocket: ~$68,600 (plus $65,850 sitting in the corp)

You "saved" ~$15,850 in current year taxes. But you haven't saved it permanently — when you eventually take that $65,850 out of the corp as dividends, you'll pay personal tax on it. The benefit is timing: that $65,850 can be invested inside the corp at the lower tax rate, compounding for years before you extract it.

⚠️Integration Isn't Perfect

While integration aims for tax neutrality, small gaps exist in both directions depending on province and income level. In some provinces, paying yourself eligible dividends actually results in slightly more total tax than the personal rate. In others, it's slightly less. These gaps change every year. Don't build a strategy around a 1-2% integration gap — it's the deferral that matters.

The Small Business Deduction: Your Corporate Tax Break

The small business deduction (SBD) is why incorporation is attractive. Here's how it works:

  • A CCPC pays a reduced combined rate (federal + provincial) on the first $500,000 of active business income per year
  • In Ontario, that combined rate is approximately 12.2% (9% federal + 3.2% provincial)
  • Compare that to the general corporate rate of 26.5% (15% federal + 11.5% provincial in Ontario) on income above $500K
  • And compare to your personal marginal rate of 43–53%+ on the same income

Combined Corporate Tax Rates by Province (SBD Rate, 2026)

  • Alberta: 11% (lowest in Canada)
  • Ontario: 12.2%
  • British Columbia: 11%
  • Quebec: 12.2%
  • Manitoba: 9% (lowest combined)
  • Saskatchewan: 10%
  • Nova Scotia: 11.5%
  • New Brunswick: 11.5%

At these rates, a corporation earning $200,000 in active business income in Ontario pays about $24,400 in corporate tax — compared to roughly $65,000+ in personal tax on the same income. The ~$40,000 difference stays invested inside the corp until you need it.

Salary vs Dividends: The Big Decision

Once you're incorporated, you need to get money out of the corporation and into your personal bank account. You have two main options:

Paying Yourself a Salary

  • Tax deductible to the corporation (reduces corporate taxable income)
  • Creates RRSP contribution room (18% of earned income)
  • Requires CPP contributions (both employee and employer portions)
  • Must run payroll — source deductions, T4 slips, remittances to CRA
  • Counts as earned income for EI purposes (though owner-managers don't qualify for regular EI)
  • Supports childcare expense deductions (requires earned income)

Paying Yourself Dividends

  • Not tax deductible to the corporation (paid from after-tax corporate income)
  • No RRSP room generated
  • No CPP contributions required — saves ~$7,700/year but means lower CPP pension at retirement
  • Simpler administration — no payroll, just declare and pay
  • Eligible dividends get the dividend tax credit, resulting in lower personal tax rates
  • No childcare expense deduction eligibility
💡The Hybrid Approach

Most accountants recommend a salary-dividend mix. Pay yourself enough salary to maximize RRSP room (~$175,000 salary generates the maximum $31,560 RRSP room in 2026) and take the rest as dividends. This gives you CPP pension credits, RRSP room, and the dividend tax credit on the remainder. The exact split depends on your province and personal situation.

Salary vs Dividends: Quick Comparison (Ontario, 2026)

Taking $100,000 as salary:

  • Personal income tax: ~$20,400
  • CPP (employee portion): ~$3,850
  • Corp pays CPP (employer portion): ~$3,850
  • RRSP room created: $18,000
  • Total cost to get $100K: ~$28,100

Taking $100,000 as eligible dividends:

  • Corporate tax already paid on $100K: ~$12,200
  • Personal tax on $100K eligible dividends: ~$14,700 (after gross-up and dividend tax credit)
  • RRSP room created: $0
  • Total cost to get $100K: ~$26,900

The dividend route looks cheaper by ~$1,200 — but you lose $18,000 of RRSP room and future CPP pension income. For most people, the salary + RRSP combination wins long-term.

Liability Protection: Not as Bulletproof as You Think

Incorporation creates a legal wall between your personal assets and business liabilities. But that wall has holes:

What Incorporation Protects Against

  • General business debts (suppliers, rent, unpaid invoices to the corp)
  • Contract disputes where the corporation is the contracting party
  • Product liability claims (in most cases)

What It Doesn't Protect Against

  • Personal guarantees: Banks and landlords almost always require you to personally guarantee business loans and leases. The corporate veil doesn't help here.
  • Professional liability: If you're a consultant giving professional advice, you can still be personally sued for negligence — incorporation doesn't shield professional malpractice.
  • CRA source deductions: Directors are personally liable for unremitted HST, payroll taxes, and source deductions.
  • Fraud or illegal activity: Courts will "pierce the corporate veil" if the corp is used to commit fraud.
  • Environmental liabilities: Directors can be personally liable.
⚠️The Insurance Reality

For most freelancers, professional liability insurance ($500–$1,500/year) provides better protection than incorporation. A $2M errors and omissions policy covers negligence claims, contract disputes, and professional mistakes — exactly the risks freelancers actually face. Incorporation is primarily a tax tool, not a liability tool.

The Real Costs of Running a Corporation

This is where the incorporation dream meets reality. A corporation is a separate legal entity with its own obligations:

One-Time Setup Costs

  • Incorporation (federal): $200 (online) or $250 (by mail)
  • Incorporation (Ontario provincial): $300+
  • Lawyer for articles + minute book: $1,000–$2,500
  • Business bank account: $0–$30/month
  • Total setup: $1,500–$3,000

Annual Ongoing Costs

  • Accountant (corporate T2 return + personal return): $2,000–$4,000/year
  • Bookkeeping: $100–$300/month ($1,200–$3,600/year) or DIY with software
  • Annual corporate filing: $12–$66 (varies by jurisdiction)
  • Payroll processing: $20–$50/month if using a service
  • Business bank fees: $0–$30/month
  • Legal (minute book updates, resolutions): $200–$500/year
  • Total annual minimum: $2,500–$5,000+

If your tax savings from incorporation are less than $5,000/year, you're paying more to maintain the structure than you're saving. This is why income level matters so much.

The Income Threshold: When Does It Make Sense?

Here's a practical decision framework based on net self-employment income (after business expenses):

Under $50,000 Net Income

Stay as a sole proprietor. Your personal tax rates are relatively low, and the costs of incorporation (~$3,000–$5,000/year) would eat any theoretical savings. Focus on maximizing business deductions and contributing to your TFSA/RRSP.

$50,000–$80,000 Net Income

Probably stay as a sole proprietor. The tax deferral benefit is modest at this level because you likely need most of the income for personal expenses. If you can't leave at least $20,000–$30,000 inside the corp annually, the math doesn't work.

$80,000–$120,000 Net Income

The grey zone. Incorporation starts to make sense if: (a) you can leave $30,000+ in the corp, (b) your income is stable and growing, and (c) you don't have significant income from other sources pushing you into high brackets anyway. Talk to an accountant about your specific numbers.

Over $120,000 Net Income

Incorporation almost certainly makes sense. The tax deferral on income above what you need to live on is substantial. At $150,000+ net, you could be deferring $15,000–$25,000 in taxes annually, which compounds significantly over time.

Holding Companies: The Next Level

Once your operating corporation accumulates significant investments, some advisors recommend a holding company (holdco) structure:

  • Operating company (opco) earns active business income and pays dividends to the holdco
  • Holding company receives those dividends tax-free (inter-corporate dividend deduction) and invests them
  • If the opco faces a lawsuit or goes bankrupt, investments in the holdco are protected
  • The holdco can also hold real estate, life insurance policies, and other passive investments

When a Holdco Makes Sense

  • Your operating company has $200,000+ in retained earnings
  • You want to protect investment assets from operating business risk
  • You're planning for estate and succession (holdco shares can be structured for tax-efficient wealth transfer)
  • You want to use the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption (LCGE) on a future sale of the operating company
📌The LCGE Opportunity

If you ever sell your corporation, the first $1,016,836 (2026) of capital gains on qualifying small business corporation shares is tax-free thanks to the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption. This is worth up to ~$270,000 in tax savings. Proper corporate structure and "purification" of the company (ensuring it meets the criteria) well before a sale is critical. A holdco can help with this.

Personal Services Business: The CRA Trap

This is the single biggest risk for incorporated freelancers, and most don't know about it until it's too late.

A Personal Services Business (PSB) exists when an incorporated individual would be considered an employee of their client if it weren't for the corporation. If CRA reclassifies your corporation as a PSB:

  • You lose the small business deduction entirely
  • Your corporate tax rate jumps to the general rate plus an additional 5% (approximately 33% in Ontario vs 12.2%)
  • Most business expenses become non-deductible (only salary, benefits to the incorporated employee, and legal fees related to collecting income remain deductible)
  • CRA can reassess previous years — the back-taxes, interest, and penalties can be devastating

PSB Risk Factors

CRA considers the same factors as employee vs contractor determinations:

  • Control: Does the client control how, when, and where you work?
  • Tools: Do you use your own equipment or the client's?
  • Financial risk: Can you profit or lose money on the engagement?
  • Integration: Are you integrated into the client's organization?
  • Exclusivity: Do you work for only one client? (Biggest red flag)
⚠️The One-Client Problem

If 80–100% of your corporate revenue comes from a single client, you're at high risk of PSB reclassification. CRA has been increasingly aggressive about auditing incorporated IT consultants and other professionals who work full-time for one company through a corporation. The fix: maintain multiple clients, use your own tools, set your own schedule, and have a genuine business operation beyond just invoicing one company.

How to Protect Yourself

  • Have more than 5 full-time employees (automatic exemption from PSB rules — unrealistic for most freelancers)
  • Maintain multiple clients simultaneously
  • Use written contracts that clearly establish a contractor relationship
  • Provide your own tools and equipment
  • Set your own hours and work location
  • Take on financial risk (fixed-fee projects vs hourly billing)
  • Keep documentation proving your independence

Decision Framework: Should You Incorporate?

✅ Incorporate if:

  • Net self-employment income consistently exceeds $100,000+
  • You can leave $30,000+ per year inside the corporation (you don't need all the income to live)
  • You have multiple clients (low PSB risk)
  • Your income is stable and growing (not a one-off good year)
  • You want to income-split with a spouse (limited after TOSI rules, but still possible in some cases)
  • You plan to sell the business someday (LCGE eligibility)
  • You have significant liability exposure beyond what insurance covers

❌ Stay sole proprietor if:

  • Net income is under $80,000
  • You spend everything you earn (no money to leave in the corp)
  • You work for essentially one client (PSB risk)
  • Your income is volatile or declining
  • You hate paperwork and don't want to pay $3,000+/year in accounting fees
  • You're planning to stop freelancing soon (winding down a corp costs money too)

How to Incorporate: The Practical Steps

If you've decided to incorporate, here's the process:

Step 1: Choose Federal vs Provincial

  • Federal incorporation ($200 via Corporations Canada online): Your name is protected nationwide. You may still need to register extra-provincially in your home province.
  • Provincial incorporation ($300+ in Ontario): Simpler if you operate in one province only. Name protection is provincial.
  • Most freelancers choose federal for broader protection and flexibility.

Step 2: Choose a Name or Number

  • Named corporation: Requires a NUANS name search report (~$14–$75). Example: "Maple Consulting Inc."
  • Numbered corporation: Free — CRA assigns a number. Example: "1234567 Canada Inc." Operates under a trade name.
  • Most solo freelancers go numbered — it's cheaper and faster.

Step 3: File Articles of Incorporation

  • Online through Corporations Canada (federal) or ServiceOntario (provincial)
  • Define share structure (typically: unlimited common shares, maybe preferred shares for future flexibility)
  • A lawyer can do this for $1,000–$2,000 or you can DIY for $200–$400

Step 4: Post-Incorporation Setup

  • Get a Business Number (BN) from CRA
  • Register for HST/GST (mandatory if revenue exceeds $30,000 in 4 consecutive quarters — likely already required)
  • Open a corporate bank account
  • Set up payroll if paying yourself salary
  • Create a minute book with organizational resolutions, share certificates, register of directors
  • Choose a fiscal year-end (doesn't have to be December 31 — pick a date that gives you flexibility for tax planning)
💡The Year-End Trick

A non-calendar fiscal year-end gives you powerful tax planning flexibility. If your corp's year-end is March 31, for example, you have until September 30 (6 months later) to file the corporate return and can plan salary/dividend distributions after you know the full-year numbers. You also separate your personal and corporate tax filing deadlines, reducing your accountant's crunch-time workload (which sometimes means lower fees).

The Bottom Line

Incorporation is a powerful tool — but it's a tool, not a magic wand. The benefits are real for freelancers earning $100,000+ who can leave money inside the corp, maintain proper corporate governance, and avoid the PSB trap. For everyone else, a well-run sole proprietorship with strategic use of TFSA, RRSP, and business deductions is simpler, cheaper, and nearly as tax-efficient.

  • The real savings come from tax deferral — investing inside the corp at 12% tax vs 45%+ personal rates
  • Integration means extraction is still taxed — you're deferring, not eliminating
  • Annual costs of $3,000–$5,000+ eat into savings for lower earners
  • PSB rules are a genuine risk for single-client freelancers
  • Get a good accountant — the cost of professional advice is the best money you'll spend. A $2,000 accountant who saves you $15,000 in properly structured tax deferral is a 650% return.

Don't incorporate because your friend did. Incorporate because the math makes sense for your income, your spending, and your long-term plans.
