Bank of Canada Holds at 2.25%: What the Rate Pause Means for Your Money

Rick Minji•March 19, 2026•11 min read

Yesterday, the Bank of Canada did exactly what markets expected — and that's precisely the problem. For the third consecutive announcement, the central bank held its overnight policy rate at 2.25%, keeping the prime rate at 4.45% and leaving millions of Canadian borrowers, savers, and homebuyers in a frustrating state of limbo.

After an aggressive cutting cycle that brought rates down 275 basis points from their 5.0% peak between June 2024 and October 2025, the Bank has been on pause since last fall. But this isn't a comfortable pause — it's a tense one. A weakening economy, an ugly jobs report, US tariff fallout, and now a war in Iran are pulling policymakers in opposite directions.

Let's unpack what this means for your mortgage, your savings, and your financial planning — and what's likely coming next.

🎯 Key Takeaway

  • Bank of Canada held at 2.25% on March 18, 2026 — the third consecutive hold after nine cuts
  • Prime rate stays at 4.45%, keeping variable mortgage rates at ~3.35% (lowest since summer 2022)
  • Canada lost 84,000 jobs in February — the worst monthly job loss since the pandemic
  • GDP grew only 1.7% in 2025 — the slowest pace since 2020
  • The Iran conflict is spiking energy prices and raising inflation fears, creating stagflation risk
  • Next decision: April 16, 2026 — markets are pricing in a hold, but risks are tilted both ways
  • For borrowers: Enjoy rate stability but prepare for potential volatility ahead
  • For savers: Lock in GIC rates now — they may drop further if the economy weakens, or rise if inflation returns

What Just Happened

On March 18, 2026, the Bank of Canada's Governing Council voted to maintain the overnight lending rate at 2.25%. This is the rate that directly influences the prime rate set by commercial banks, which in turn determines pricing for variable-rate mortgages, HELOCs, and other floating-rate products.

In its accompanying statement, the Bank acknowledged it's caught between two competing risks:

"With recent data pointing to weaker economic activity and uncertainty elevated, risks to growth look tilted to the downside. At the same time, inflation risks have gone up due to higher energy prices."

Translation: the economy is weak enough to justify cutting rates, but inflation pressure from rising energy prices is strong enough to prevent it. The Bank is stuck.

📌What Does "Overnight Rate" Actually Mean?

The overnight rate is the interest rate at which major banks lend money to each other for one-day periods. It's the Bank of Canada's primary tool for influencing the economy. When the BoC raises it, borrowing gets more expensive across the board. When it lowers it, borrowing gets cheaper. The prime rate (currently 4.45%) is typically the overnight rate plus 2.20 percentage points.

The Rate Journey: From 5% Peak to 2.25% Pause

To understand where we are, you need to understand how we got here. The Bank of Canada's rate path over the past three years has been one of the most dramatic in its history.

Nine rate cuts totaling 275 basis points brought significant relief to variable-rate borrowers. A homeowner with a $500,000 variable mortgage saw their rate drop from roughly 6.7% to 3.35% — saving approximately $1,100 per month. But the cutting cycle appears to be over, at least for now.

What This Means for Your Mortgage

Variable-Rate Mortgages

If you have a variable-rate mortgage, the rate hold means no change to your payments. Your rate remains tied to prime (4.45%), and the best variable rates available are around 3.35% (prime minus 1.10%). That's the lowest variable rate since the summer of 2022, before the Bank's hiking cycle reached its peak.

The good news: you've already benefited enormously from nine cuts. The risk: depending on the type of variable mortgage you hold, future rate movements could go either way.

  • Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM): Your payment changes with each rate move. No change now means stable payments.
  • Variable-rate mortgage (VRM) with fixed payments: Your payment stays the same, but the interest/principal split shifts. At current rates, more is going to principal than during the peak.
⚠️Variable-Rate Borrowers: Don't Get Complacent

If the Iran conflict drives sustained inflation, the Bank could be forced to hike rates again — even with a weak economy. This is the stagflation scenario. If you're on a variable rate, make sure you can absorb at least a 1–2% rate increase. Use FiggyBank's Mortgage Calculator to stress-test your payments.

Fixed-Rate Mortgages

Fixed rates are driven by bond yields, not the overnight rate directly. As of mid-March 2026, the best 5-year fixed rates are hovering around 3.89%–4.19%, while 3-year fixed rates are in the 3.69%–3.99% range.

Fixed rates have actually ticked up slightly in recent weeks due to rising bond yields driven by the Iran conflict and energy price uncertainty. If you're renewing soon and want certainty, locking in now may be prudent — the geopolitical situation could push fixed rates higher.

Mortgage Renewals in 2026

Roughly 1.2 million Canadian mortgages are up for renewal this year. If you locked in a 5-year fixed rate in 2021 at ~1.89%, you're renewing into a rate environment of 3.89%–4.19% — a significant jump, but far less painful than it would have been a year ago when rates were above 5%.

Should You Lock in a GIC Now?

This is the million-dollar question, and the answer depends on which risk worries you more:

  • If the economy weakens further and the BoC cuts again, GIC rates will drop. Locking in now protects you.
  • If inflation returns and the BoC hikes, GIC rates will rise. Waiting (or using short-term GICs) keeps you flexible.

Given the current uncertainty, a GIC ladder remains the smartest approach. Spread your money across 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year terms. You'll capture current rates while maintaining regular liquidity to reinvest at whatever rates prevail in the future.

💡Pro Tip: HISA vs GIC Right Now

With HISAs offering 3.00%–3.50% and 1-year GICs at 3.25%–3.75%, the spread is narrow. If you might need the money within 12 months, a HISA gives you virtually the same return with full liquidity. Only lock into GICs for money you genuinely won't touch — and even then, a cashable GIC (typically 0.25%–0.50% lower) may be worth the flexibility premium.

How the Rate Pause Affects the Housing Market

Canada's housing market remains in a holding pattern that mirrors the Bank's rate stance. According to the Canadian Real Estate Association (CREA), national home sales slipped 1.3% month over month in February 2026, extending a trend of muted activity.

The GTA market has been particularly cautious, with both buyers and sellers waiting for clearer signals on rates and the economy before making moves.

Why the Market Feels Stuck

  • Buyers are waiting for lower rates — many believe further cuts are coming and want to maximize purchasing power
  • Sellers are holding firm on prices — with limited inventory, there's less pressure to discount
  • Tariff uncertainty is dampening confidence, especially for buyers in manufacturing-dependent regions
  • Job losses (84,000 in February) are making buyers cautious about taking on large mortgages

The spring market — traditionally the busiest period for Canadian real estate — may be quieter than usual this year. For buyers, that could mean less competition and more negotiating power. For sellers, patience may be required.

📌Spring 2026 Housing Market Outlook

The new mortgage rules that took effect in late 2024 — including 30-year amortizations for first-time buyers and the $1.5M insured mortgage cap — should provide a floor for demand. But with economic uncertainty at elevated levels, don't expect the frenzy of 2021–2022. The market favours patient, well-prepared buyers right now.

The Stagflation Risk: Why the Bank of Canada Is Stuck

The word "stagflation" hasn't been a mainstream concern in Canada since the 1970s. But in March 2026, it's back — and it's the reason the Bank of Canada can't move in either direction.

What Is Stagflation?

Stagflation occurs when an economy experiences stagnant growth (or contraction) combined with rising inflation. It's the worst of both worlds: you have a weak economy that would normally justify lower interest rates, but inflation that prevents the central bank from cutting.

Why Stagflation Is on the Table

Three forces are colliding to create this risk in Canada:

  1. Weak growth: GDP expanded just 1.7% in 2025, the slowest since 2020. Canada lost 84,000 jobs in February alone — the worst monthly decline since the pandemic. Consumer confidence is low.
  2. US tariff impacts: Trade tensions have disrupted supply chains, particularly in auto manufacturing and agriculture. Our recent analysis details how tariffs are pushing up consumer prices while dampening export-driven employment.
  3. The Iran conflict: The emerging war in Iran has sent global energy prices sharply higher. Oil above $100/barrel translates directly into higher gasoline, heating, and food transportation costs for Canadians. This is cost-push inflation — the kind central banks can't easily control.
⚠️The Stagflation Trap

In a normal recession, the Bank of Canada cuts rates to stimulate growth. In a normal inflation surge, it hikes rates to cool demand. Stagflation removes both options. Cutting rates would fuel inflation; hiking rates would deepen the recession. The Bank is essentially stuck at 2.25% until one risk clearly dominates the other. The last time Canada experienced genuine stagflation was in the 1970s and early 1980s, when the overnight rate ultimately peaked above 20%.

What Stagflation Means for Your Money

If stagflation materializes, the effects ripple through every aspect of personal finance:

  • Savings lose purchasing power: A 3.25% GIC doesn't help much if inflation is running at 4–5%
  • Wages stagnate: Weak labour markets mean limited raises, while prices rise
  • Housing stalls: Higher inflation expectations can push bond yields (and fixed mortgage rates) higher, even as the BoC holds or cuts the overnight rate
  • Equities get volatile: Markets hate stagflation because companies face rising costs with limited pricing power

The silver lining: stagflation is a risk, not a certainty. The Bank is explicitly watching the Iran conflict's duration and impact. If energy prices stabilize, the stagflation threat recedes, and the door reopens for further rate cuts in the second half of 2026.

Three Possible Scenarios

Scenario 1: Another hold (most likely). If CPI stays in the 2.5%–3.0% range and the labour market stabilizes, the Bank will likely hold again in April. Markets are pricing this as the base case.

Scenario 2: A rate cut. If March data shows continued job losses and manageable inflation, the Bank could cut 25 bps to 2.00% to support the economy. This would require the Iran conflict to remain contained and energy prices to stabilize.

Scenario 3: A rate hike. The least likely but most disruptive scenario. If CPI spikes above 3.5% and the Bank decides it cannot tolerate rising inflation expectations, it could hike — even with a weak economy. This would be a stagflationary response and would shock markets.

💡Watch the Bond Market

Fixed mortgage rates are driven by Government of Canada bond yields, not the overnight rate. Even if the BoC holds, bond yields can move independently based on inflation expectations and global risk appetite. If you're shopping for a fixed-rate mortgage, monitor the 5-year GoC bond yield — when it rises, fixed mortgage rates follow within days.

What You Should Do Right Now

Uncertainty is uncomfortable, but it doesn't mean inaction. Here's a practical checklist based on your situation:

If You Have a Variable-Rate Mortgage

  • Enjoy the stability — your rate is at its lowest point in four years
  • Stress-test your budget — can you handle a 1–2% increase if inflation forces a hike?
  • Consider locking in if you have low risk tolerance — many lenders allow you to convert to fixed mid-term
  • Accelerate payments — if your budget allows, use this low-rate window to pay down principal faster

If You're Renewing Your Mortgage

  • Start shopping 120 days before renewal — never accept your lender's first offer
  • Get rate holds from multiple lenders — a 120-day hold protects you if rates rise
  • Consider a shorter term (2–3 years) — if you believe rates will eventually fall further, a shorter term lets you renew into a potentially better environment
  • Read our Mortgage Renewal Strategies guide for the full playbook

If You're a Saver or Investor

  • Build a GIC ladder — spread across 1-, 2-, 3-, and 5-year terms for rate diversification
  • Keep 3–6 months expenses liquid in a HISA — economic uncertainty makes emergency funds critical
  • Don't chase yield — in uncertain environments, capital preservation matters more than maximizing returns
  • Review your asset allocation — stagflation historically favours real assets (commodities, real estate, TIPS) over bonds and growth stocks

If You're a First-Time Home Buyer

  • Get pre-approved now — a pre-approval locks in today's rates for 90–120 days
  • A quiet spring market is your friend — less competition means more negotiating power
  • Max out your FHSA — $8,000 per year in tax-deductible, tax-free savings for your down payment
  • Don't try to time the bottom — waiting for the "perfect" rate often means missing the right home

The Bottom Line

The Bank of Canada's third consecutive rate hold at 2.25% reflects an economy caught between opposing forces: weak growth that wants lower rates, and rising inflation risks that prevent them. For Canadians, this means a period of rate stability but economic uncertainty.

The nine rate cuts from June 2024 to October 2025 delivered meaningful relief — variable mortgage rates dropped from 6.7% to 3.35%, saving the average borrower over $1,000 per month. But with stagflation risk lurking, further cuts are not guaranteed, and the possibility of future hikes — however unlikely — cannot be ruled out.

The best response isn't to try to predict the Bank's next move. It's to prepare for multiple scenarios: stress-test your mortgage, build your savings buffer, diversify your investments, and make decisions based on what you can control rather than what you can't.
